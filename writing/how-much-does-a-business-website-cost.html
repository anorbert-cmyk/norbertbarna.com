How much does a website cost? What the price depends on

How much does a website cost for a business? The nine factors behind the price, a real example and the questions to ask before a quote.

Norbert Barna12 November 20269 min read

How much does a website cost? It depends on what the website has to do: who it is for, what a visitor should do on it, and which systems it connects to. Two quotes for the same request can differ many times over, and often both are fair, because they do not price the same work. I treat every website as custom work: technology, structure, design and content follow from what your users need, and so does the price.

There are no numbers in this article. I go through the nine factors that move the price of a custom business website the most, show an example from my own work, and describe how to ask for a quote you can compare with another.

Why I don’t put a fixed price on a website

I believe in quality, not volume. A website from me is not a filled-in template. It is work that shows your brand, your products and my craft as they really are. So the price follows from what has to be built, not from the number of pages.

I work on projects where the client puts energy in as well: provides content, decides, gives feedback. A custom website is joint work. If you need a quickly filled template, that can be a good decision, but then I am not the best partner for it, and I say so in the first conversation.

Why you get very different quotes for the same request

Two quotes rarely price the same thing. One sets up a template with your copy. The other starts with discovery, plans the visitor’s path, and builds the system that goes with it. Both are sold as “web development services”. The question is not which one is cheaper, but what is in the price. If neither says what the site has to achieve, they probably describe two different websites.

What does the price of a business website depend on? Nine factors

1. What the site has to achieve

A brochure site proves that the company exists and can be trusted. A lead generation site has to be found by people searching for your service, and has to lead them to get in touch. An online shop handles products, stock and payment. A web application is something users log into and work in. Each of the four is a different job.

2. Content: who writes it, and how much there is

Copy and photos are often more work than the code. Finished copy just gets built in. Copy that has to be written or rewritten is skilled work: an interview with you, understanding the offer, editing, rounds of approval. A second language is not a translate button: every page exists twice, with its own title, description and search optimisation.

3. Custom design or a template

A ready-made template launches fast, and for many companies it is enough. Custom design pays off when visitors need to trust you before they decide, or when the content does not fit a template’s structure. It is more than the look: it covers navigation, the mobile view, forms and error states.

4. Features and integrations

Every feature that talks to another system brings its own work and its own failure cases. A contact form needs a decision on where the message goes and how spam is filtered. Online payment needs the successful, the failed and the abandoned payment handled. A member area needs login, permissions, and paid content that really stays protected. CRM, invoicing, newsletter, booking calendar: wherever data moves, the error handling has to be designed too.

5. Technology that fits your users

A static site, a site on a content management system, or a web application: the choice comes from what your visitors and your team need, not from habit. In my web development work the technology is fitted to the users too. A brochure site that rarely changes does not need the system a paid membership platform needs, and a web application should not be forced into a site builder.

6. Content management after launch

Who changes the site after launch? If it rarely changes, the developer can make the edits. If new articles, offers or courses appear regularly, you need a content management system your team works in without a developer, and will actually use. Ask to be shown it before handover.

7. Privacy and security

If the site measures visits, the GDPR requires the visitor’s consent, and the measurement has to stay off until the visitor has decided. You need a privacy notice, and a form has to say where the data goes. For health or financial topics the bar is higher: security has to live in the code, for example as a strict content security policy, not on a policy page.

8. Accessibility, findability, speed

The European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, has applied since 28 June 2025 to certain services, for example e-commerce. Microenterprises providing services are exempt. Whether it applies to you is a question for a lawyer. This is a product designer’s reading, not legal advice. Keyboard navigation and sufficient contrast belong to a good site either way.

A finished site does not appear in Google by itself. Fast loading, server-side rendering, a unique title and description per page, structured data, a sitemap and marked-up language versions are cheap while building and expensive afterwards. Ask what the quote means by search optimisation: the technical foundations, or keyword research and a content plan as well. Those are two different jobs.

9. Operations, maintenance, deadline

The price does not end at launch. There are domain and hosting fees, the content management system and its dependencies need updates, backups have to exist, and someone has to notice when something goes down. A tight launch date rearranges the work: shorter feedback rounds, and often some features move to a second round.

An example: what went into the Kineticare platform

Two physiotherapists who specialise in hand and arm rehabilitation wanted to bring the same method to patients who will never sit in their treatment room. I designed and built it: brand, content, interface, a Next.js platform, Payload as the content management system (the physiotherapists publish without a developer), course videos streamed from a CDN, Barion payment, a member area for paid content, GDPR consent management and a strict content security policy.

The lesson for the price: the “website” here was in fact a product. The work was in the patient journey, the payment and the member access, not in the number of pages. The details are in the Kineticare case study.

How to ask for a quote on a custom website

  1. Describe in one paragraph who the site is for and what a visitor should do on it: call, pay, sign up.
  2. List the features using the factors above, and mark what is needed for launch and what can wait.
  3. Write down the systems you already have: invoicing, CRM, booking calendar, newsletter tool.
  4. Decide who writes the copy and who manages the site after launch.
  5. Ask for a quote that states, item by item, what is included, what is not, and how maintenance works.

Then put the same questions to everyone who quotes, me included:

  • What is the goal of the site, and what counts as success?
  • Are copywriting, photography and translation included?
  • Template or custom design? How many design rounds?
  • Who changes the site after launch, and how?
  • Which integrations are included, and what happens when an external service does not respond?
  • What does the search optimisation cover?
  • What does running the site cost per month or per year, and who is responsible when something breaks?
  • Who owns the source code, the domain and the content?

What is usually not included in the price of a website?

In my experience, ongoing content production, paid advertising, photography (unless stated separately) and third-party fees such as hosting, the domain, the payment provider’s commission and the email system are usually not part of the price. This is an observation, not market data, so always ask.

Where to start

The price of a website is a consequence of what you expect from it. If you want it to bring in clients and show your brand as it really is, then content, trust and measurement are part of the work. Say that in the first conversation. Once the five steps are written down, send them over and I will tell you what you need for launch.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a website cost for a small business?

The range is wide, because the word “website” does not describe the same work every time. Every website I build is custom work, so the price follows from the task: the content, the features, the integrations and the maintenance. With the five steps above written down, I can give you a precise quote, and you can compare two quotes for the same work.

Is a website built from a template cheaper?

At launch, usually yes. Customisation and later extensions can make it more expensive if the template’s structure does not fit your visitors. For many companies a ready-made template is enough.

How long does it take to build a website?

It depends mostly on the content and the number of features, not on the code. Once the scope is itemised I can name a date. I don’t promise a general number of weeks.

What is the difference between a website and a web application?

Once it includes login, payment or data processing, it is an application. That needs different design, testing and operations. I write more about it on the web development page.

Does my business website have to be accessible?

The European Accessibility Act has applied to certain services since June 2025, and microenterprises providing services are exempt. Whether it applies to you is a question for your lawyer. Keyboard use and good contrast are part of a good site regardless.

Do you work with companies outside Hungary?

Yes. I work with clients in Hungary, Germany and other EU countries. The work runs in English or Hungarian.
