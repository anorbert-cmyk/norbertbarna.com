EU AI Act Article 50: what product teams must label now

EU AI Act Article 50 in practice: what has applied since August 2026, what the Digital Omnibus changed, and how to design the label.

Norbert Barna12 November 20268 min read

Contents (6)

The transparency obligations in Article 50 of the EU AI Act have applied since 2 August 2026. In short: if you run an AI system that interacts directly with people, you must tell those people, unless it is obvious from the circumstances. If you generate or publish AI-generated content, you must label it in certain cases, in particular deepfakes and machine-generated content. The 2026 Digital Omnibus moved many of the regulation’s deadlines, but not these duties.

I wrote this article for product teams, not for lawyers. It describes what the text asks for as I read it as a product lead, and above all how to design the disclosure so that it builds trust instead of being ignored like a warning sticker. It is not legal advice; which duty applies to your product is a question for your counsel.

On working together: I work with teams across the EU, and the work runs in English or Hungarian.

What does Article 50 of the EU AI Act require?

Article 50 contains several transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems. The AI disclosure requirements and the AI labelling requirements that people ask about are two sides of the same article. Read as a product team, it comes down to four cases:

  1. Interaction with people. An AI system intended to interact directly with people (a customer service chatbot, for example) must be designed so that the person learns they are interacting with an AI, unless this is obvious to a reasonable person from the circumstances.
  2. Synthetic content. Systems that artificially generate audio, image, video or text must mark their outputs in a machine-readable form, so that they can be recognised as artificially generated or manipulated.
  3. Deepfakes. Anyone who generates or alters image, audio or video content that deceptively resembles real people, places or events must disclose that it was artificially generated or manipulated.
  4. Published AI text. Anyone who publishes AI-generated text to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose that, unless a person has reviewed the text and someone holds editorial responsibility for it.

These duties have applied since 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, in force since 27 July 2026) moved the obligations for high-risk systems under Annex III to 2 December 2027 and under Annex I to 2 August 2028, and it softened the AI literacy duty from “ensure” to “promote”. The transparency obligations in Article 50 stayed as they were (sources: Cooley (opens in a new tab), National Law Review (opens in a new tab), accessed 21 October 2026).

Two limits on this summary. For the exact exceptions, such as purposes authorised by law or content that is obviously artistic, the text of the regulation counts, not this article. And whether your system also falls under the high-risk rules is a separate question with its own timetable.

Why the label is a design task

A sentence like “This answer was generated by AI” may satisfy the duty on paper. Whether it achieves anything depends on whether the person reads it, understands it and makes a better decision because of it.

At Instructure I led feature design for AI features in a learning platform that several million people used every day. Clear AI labelling was a guardrail there from day one, long before the regulation applied. The reason was not the law. Education, government and healthcare customers buy trust, and a label that says honestly what the AI did was part of that trust. The project is written up in the Instructure case study.

What we learned there fits into five decisions.

Five decisions on AI labelling

1. Where the label sits, and whether it stays

The label belongs on the output, not in the footer and not in the terms of use. Decide as well what happens when a person edits the output. Does the label disappear, does it become “suggested by AI, edited by a person”, or does it stay unchanged? Each of those answers can be defended. Having no answer is the problem.

2. What the label says

“AI-generated” says very little. It is more useful to say what the AI did: summarised, translated, wrote a draft, calculated a recommendation. At Instructure the principle was “explanation before suggestion”: the signals and sources behind a suggestion came before the suggestion itself. That is labelling that helps a person decide.

3. The moment of disclosure in a conversation

In customer service the notice belongs at the start of the conversation, not in the third reply, and it should be repeated when a person takes over. At every point the customer should know whether they are talking to an AI or to a person, and how to reach a person.

4. Labelling and human review belong together

An output that is labelled as AI-generated but can be neither edited nor rejected produces distrust, not trust. At Instructure every AI suggestion had a label, an edit state and a way back, and nothing the model decided was allowed to stay invisible. I describe the patterns behind this in human-in-the-loop AI for regulated products.

5. What gets logged

For the duty to mark synthetic content in a machine-readable form, and for later reviews, it must be possible to trace which output was generated by which system and who edited it. That is a decision about the data model and the logging. Make it before you build, not after the first request from a supervisory authority.

What this means for small and mid-sized companies

Most small and mid-sized companies are affected by Article 50 in exactly one of two ways: they use an AI system that talks to customers, or they publish AI-generated content. Both can be handled with the five decisions above inside a pilot project, without building a legal department.

What matters is bringing legal advice in early. At Instructure, Legal, Compliance and Data Science co-wrote the guardrails from the start. They did not inspect them at the end.

A checklist to begin with:

  • Which of our AI features interact directly with people?
  • Which of them generate text, image, audio or video that gets published?
  • Where and when does the person learn that AI is involved?
  • Can the person edit the output, reject it or reach a human?
  • What is logged, and who can stop the feature?

If you are about to bring in outside help for this, what to prepare before hiring an AI integration partner lists the inputs worth having ready.

Where to start

For a well-designed product the Article 50 duties are not an extra burden. They are the legal form of something users expect anyway: to know what they are dealing with. If you design the label, the edit state and the way back from the beginning, you meet the duty along the way.

Frequently asked questions

When do the EU AI Act transparency obligations apply?

The transparency obligations in Article 50 have applied since 2 August 2026. The 2026 Digital Omnibus did not postpone them.

Does every chatbot have to say it is an AI?

An AI system intended to interact directly with people must inform those people, unless it is obvious from the circumstances. Whether that exception covers your case is a question for your counsel.

Does Article 50 apply to AI tools used only internally?

Article 50 is aimed at interaction with people and at published content. An internal assistant whose outputs are checked by employees before anything goes out is a different case, and what an employer owes its own staff is a separate question. This is not legal advice.

What did the 2026 Digital Omnibus change in the AI Act?

It moved the deadlines for high-risk systems, Annex III to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028, and it softened the AI literacy duty. The labelling and transparency obligations in Article 50 remained in place.

Is a notice in the terms of use enough for AI disclosure?

From a product point of view, no. The duty is aimed at the person being informed at the moment of interaction. Whether a specific implementation is legally sufficient is a question for your counsel; as a design matter the label belongs on the output.

Does AI-generated text have to be labelled?

Systems that generate text must mark their outputs in a machine-readable form. A visible disclosure is required when AI-generated text is published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless a person has reviewed it and someone holds editorial responsibility. For the exact scope and exceptions, the text of the regulation counts.
