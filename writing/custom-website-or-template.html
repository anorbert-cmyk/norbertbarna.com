Custom website vs template: six criteria to decide

Custom website vs template for a small business: six criteria, a decision table and a real example to help you choose.

In the custom website vs template decision, a template is the better choice for a small business whose site mainly informs: what you do, where you are, how to reach you. A custom website is the better choice when the site has to earn trust before someone pays or enquires, when it includes payment, booking or a member area, when it has to exchange data with your other systems, or when search visibility decides whether you get work. Price is the last criterion in this decision, not the first.

This article explains the difference, gives six criteria with examples from my own projects, and ends with a decision table. I work with companies across the EU, in English or Hungarian.

What is the difference between a template and a custom website?

A website template is a finished structure and a finished look that you fill with your own content. On a hosted website builder it comes as a rental package: software, hosting and updates in one subscription. A template site launches quickly and you can maintain it yourself. Its limits are the limits of the template and the provider: if the template has three service cards, you will have three, even if you need one or seven, and moving to another system usually means starting again.

A custom website starts from the visitor’s path. Structure, design, content, features and technology are made for what your customers need to decide, not for what a layout provides. You own the result and can extend it. A custom website takes longer, costs more at the start, and needs someone to build it.

Between the two sits a customised template: an open system such as WordPress, or a modern content management system, with a ready-made base and structural and content decisions made for your business. For many small businesses the customised template is the right answer.

When is a custom website worth it for a small business? Six criteria

  1. Trust before payment or enquiry. A hairdresser can use an attractive template, because the customer will see the salon anyway. For a consultancy, a clinic or a law firm, the website is the first proof of quality. The structure then has to follow the visitor’s doubts, and no template knows where those doubts are.
  2. Payment, booking or a member area. Website builders offer shops and bookings inside their own model. Once the flow differs from that model, for example a free introductory course that leads to a paid programme with protected content, the site is an application. An application has to handle the successful, the failed and the abandoned payment, and keep paid content protected.
  3. Connections to other systems. CRM, invoicing, booking calendar, newsletter tool: every integration brings its own failure cases, such as an external service that does not answer or a form that arrives twice. An AI assistant on the site is an integration too, and I describe its failure cases in AI hallucinations in business.
  4. Who manages the content after launch. Wanting to edit the site yourself does not automatically point to a template. A custom site with a well-chosen content management system is just as editable by your own team. Ask to be shown the editing interface before handover and try it.
  5. How much the site will grow. Five pages that stay the same for two years are a case for a template. A site that will add languages, product lines, locations or a knowledge section within a year needs consistent components and a content model, and those are designed at the start, not retrofitted.
  6. Search visibility and speed. If customers should find you through Google or AI assistants, loading time, clean structure, a unique title and description per page, structured data and accessibility count. With a template, check that each of these can be set. With a custom site they are designed for your pages.

An example: why Kineticare could not be a template

Kineticare is a digital health platform for physiotherapist-led hand rehabilitation at home, which I designed and built. Four of the six criteria applied at once.

  • Trust. A person with months of wrist pain has already followed advice that led nowhere, so the site is built around what a patient can verify: who the two physiotherapists are, what the treatment involves step by step, and what happened to other patients with the same complaint. Photographs of real hands replace stock imagery, and patient stories sit next to the sections that create hesitation.
  • Payment and member area. A free course sits at the entrance and paid programmes follow. Payments go through Barion, course video streams from a CDN, and a member area gates the paid content.
  • Content management. Payload is the content system, so the two physiotherapists publish articles and course material without a developer.
  • Search. Pages render on the server, which keeps them fast and fully readable by search engines.

The “website” was in fact a product. The details are in the Kineticare case study, and the same reasoning for a first version is in MVP development for regulated products.

The growth criterion shows at a larger scale in the Raiffeisen case study: interface and processes differed across 16 countries, and a “rebuild vs. patch” workshop led to a full redesign on a unified design system. A small business faces the same question with lower stakes.

Should you build the website yourself or have it built?

Building the site yourself makes sense when you have the time and the site stays small. Having it built makes sense when your hours are worth more in the business than in a site builder, and when the site has to win customers, not merely exist. What belongs in a quote, and which factors the price follows from, is in How much does a website cost?.

I do not fill templates. Every site in my web development work starts with who visits and what that person should decide. If the six criteria say a template is enough for you, I say so in the first conversation.

Where to start

The choice between a custom website and a template is settled by what the site has to do for your customers, not by taste. Write down in three sentences who visits your website and what should happen there, mark which of the six criteria apply, and send it over. Frequently asked questions

Is a custom website or a template better for a small business?

A template is better when the site only has to inform and you want to maintain it yourself. A custom website is better when the site has to build trust before a purchase, take payments or bookings in your own flow, connect to other systems, or bring in customers through search.

Is a custom website more expensive than a template?

At the start, usually yes. In operation, not necessarily: a hosted template is a running subscription that grows with each extension, while a custom site is built once and then needs hosting and maintenance. I do not name a fixed price, because every site is custom work.

Can I start with a template and switch to a custom website later?

Yes, and for many new businesses it is the right order. Start on your own domain, so content and URLs can move with you. At the switch you take copy and images along, not the design or the structure, and you plan redirects so search visibility is not lost.

Is WordPress a template or a custom website?

WordPress is open software that you can run with any host and extend freely, so responsibility for updates and security lies with you or your developer. WordPress with a purchased theme is closer to a template site than to a custom website.

Does a website built with AI count as a template?

If an AI tool fills in a ready-made layout, yes. If AI speeds up design and coding while a person decides who the site is for and what the visitor should do, no. The difference is who makes the decisions, not which tool wrote the code.

Who owns the website and the code?

On a hosted website builder you rent the use, and the platform stays with the provider. With a custom website the source code, domain, hosting and content should be yours in writing, as part of the quote and the handover. Keep the domain, hosting and services in your own accounts, not the developer’s.
